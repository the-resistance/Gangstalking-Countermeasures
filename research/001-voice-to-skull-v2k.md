# Voice-to-Skull (V2K)
## Observations, Proposed Technologies, and the Search for Answers

**Document Type:** Independent Research Manual  
**Version:** 0.1 — Working Draft  
**Date:** November 3, 2026  
**Author:** Kevin Mahan  
**Status:** Preliminary Research and Hypothesis Development

---

# 1. Introduction: Why I Started Investigating

My name is Kevin.

There was a time when I would read about people describing something
called Voice-to-Skull technology, commonly abbreviated as V2K, and
think they were completely off their rocker.

The concept seemed too far-fetched to take seriously.

My perspective began changing when I started noticing unusual
experiences involving sounds, voices, environmental noise, and
recordings that I could not readily explain.

I began questioning whether some of these experiences could involve
technologies that were unfamiliar to me.

One difficulty with investigating unusual experiences is that people
who have never encountered something similar may struggle to
understand what another person is describing.

Personal observations cannot always be communicated with the same
clarity as physical measurements.

However, unfamiliarity with an experience does not establish that
the experience is impossible, nor does experiencing something
establish a particular explanation.

This manual is intended to bridge that gap.

Its purpose is to document observations, examine relevant scientific
research, develop testable hypotheses, and establish procedures
that other investigators can independently reproduce.

# 2. Statement of the Problem

## 2.1 Voice Impersonation and Misrepresentation

Modern technology can generate synthetic speech, imitate vocal
characteristics, and produce convincing artificial conversations.

These capabilities raise legitimate questions about the potential
misuse of synthetic audio.

If fabricated speech is mistaken for an authentic conversation,
the consequences could include:

- False accusations
- Damaged personal relationships
- Reputational harm
- Misinterpretation of another person's intentions
- Psychological distress
- Decisions based on inaccurate information
- Escalation of interpersonal conflicts

A central concern of this investigation is whether some reported
experiences involving apparently familiar voices could be explained
by synthetic audio, environmental acoustics, electronic interference,
or other identifiable mechanisms.

No particular mechanism or intentional actor is presumed.

## 2.2 Potential Behavioral Consequences

A person who believes they have heard a familiar individual making
threatening, insulting, or incriminating statements might react
to information that was never actually spoken by that individual.

This creates an important investigative question:

**How can an investigator establish whether perceived speech
originated from an identifiable human speaker, an electronic
recording, a synthetic source, or an auditory artifact?**

The answer requires independent evidence rather than assumptions
about the speaker's identity.

# 3. Background: Relevant Technologies

The term "Voice-to-Skull" is used in various discussions to describe
the alleged delivery of speech directly into human auditory perception.

It is not itself a standardized scientific classification.

Several documented technologies and phenomena are relevant to
investigating these claims.

## 3.1 Microwave Auditory Effect

Also called the Frey effect.

Pulsed radio-frequency energy can produce auditory sensations
under certain experimentally established conditions.

Historical research includes work by Allan H. Frey and subsequent
investigators.

The existence of this effect does not establish that a particular
reported experience was caused by microwave exposure.

## 3.2 Electromagnetic Interference

Electronic devices can sometimes unintentionally detect or
demodulate electromagnetic signals.

Possible manifestations include:

- Unexpected audio from speakers
- Radio-frequency interference
- Electrical noise
- Modulation artifacts
- Distortion involving electronic equipment

## 3.3 Directional Audio

Directional loudspeaker systems can concentrate audible sound
within a relatively narrow region.

Some systems employ ultrasonic carriers and nonlinear acoustic
propagation to generate audible sound.

## 3.4 Synthetic Speech and Voice Cloning

Artificial intelligence systems can generate speech resembling
human voices.

Depending on the system, they may support:

- Multiple synthetic speakers
- Voice imitation
- Scripted dialogue
- Conversational responses
- Speech recognition
- Context-dependent dialogue generation

These are established computational capabilities.

Their use in any specific incident requires separate verification.

## 3.5 Audio Processing and Perceptual Effects

High microphone gain, compression, automatic gain control,
electrical interference, and background noise can produce
unexpected recording artifacts.

Human auditory perception can also interpret ambiguous sounds
as recognizable speech.

Both electronic and perceptual mechanisms must be considered
when evaluating unusual recordings.

# 4. Proposed Terminology and Working Hypotheses

The following terms are descriptive classifications developed
for this research manual.

They are not presented as established scientific diagnoses
or proof of an underlying technology.

Their purpose is to make observations easier to document,
compare, and test.

## 4.1 Synthetic Conversational Simulation (SCS)

**Definition:**

A proposed model in which a computational system generates
multiple synthetic voices participating in an apparently
continuous conversation.

**Reported observation:**

Speech-like sounds sometimes appear to involve several parties
taking turns, responding to one another, or participating in
a developing scenario.

**Research questions:**

- Can individual speakers be distinguished objectively?
- Does the dialogue contain repeatable sequences?
- Can independent listeners identify the same words?
- Does the apparent conversation persist across recording devices?
- Is there evidence of an external audio source?

## 4.2 Conversational Role Simulation (CRS)

**Definition:**

A proposed component of Synthetic Conversational Simulation
in which different voices appear to represent distinct
personalities, characters, or social roles.

**Reported observation:**

Different apparent speakers may seem to participate in a
coordinated conversation.

**Research objective:**

Determine whether identifiable speech is present and whether
speaker changes can be independently verified.

## 4.3 Synthetic Vocal Identity Misattribution (SVIM)

**Definition:**

A situation in which perceived or recorded speech appears
to resemble a recognizable individual, but the identity
of the actual source has not been established.

**Reported observation:**

Some sounds have appeared to resemble voices of people
personally known to the observer.

**Research questions:**

- Is intelligible speech actually present?
- Do independent listeners identify the same words?
- Can voice characteristics be measured?
- Is there evidence supporting the identity of the speaker?
- Could the resemblance arise from ambiguous audio?

**Important distinction:**

A familiar-sounding voice does not independently establish
that the familiar person participated in the recording.

## 4.4 Perceived Contextual Audio Responsiveness (PCAR)

**Definition:**

An observation in which speech-like sounds appear temporally
related to the observer's actions, activities, or surroundings.

**Reported observation:**

Certain sounds have appeared to coincide with actions
such as using electronic equipment or moving between environments.

**Research objective:**

Determine whether a statistically repeatable relationship exists
between an independently recorded event and the audio.

## 4.5 Environmental Audio Coupling (EAC)

**Definition:**

A working category for investigating apparent relationships
between environmental equipment and recorded or perceived audio.

**Equipment of interest:**

- Electric motors
- Air-conditioning systems
- Ventilation equipment
- Vehicle engines
- Electrical appliances
- Audio equipment

**Possible mechanisms to investigate:**

- Mechanical vibration
- Acoustic resonance
- Electrical interference
- Electromagnetic interference
- Background-noise modulation
- Recording artifacts

## 4.6 Environmental Speech-like Pattern Formation (ESPF)

**Definition:**

The perception of speech-like patterns within environmental noise.

**Research objective:**

Determine whether recognizable speech is present in the
recorded waveform or whether ambiguous noise is being
interpreted as speech.

## 4.7 High-Gain Vocal-like Artifacts (HGVA)

**Definition:**

Speech-like sounds that become noticeable when microphone
gain, playback volume, or digital amplification is increased.

**Reported observation:**

Faint vocal-like material has appeared during amplified
playback of recordings.

**Research objective:**

Determine whether amplification reveals an existing acoustic
signal, electronic interference, processing artifacts,
or ambiguous background noise.

# 5. Personal Field Observations

The following observations are recorded as personal reports.

Their underlying causes have not yet been independently established.

## 5.1 Environmental Machinery

Speech-like sounds have sometimes appeared in environments
containing electric motors, ventilation systems, or
air-conditioning equipment.

The apparent characteristics may change depending on
the surrounding equipment and background noise.

## 5.2 Amplified Recordings

Recordings made with an iPad or similar device have sometimes
contained faint sounds that appear more speech-like when
microphone gain or playback volume is increased.

## 5.3 Bluetooth Audio Playback

Some unusual audio characteristics have been noticed while
listening through JBL Bluetooth headphones.

Potential effects from Bluetooth transmission, audio codecs,
playback processing, and source recordings remain untested.

## 5.4 Familiar-Sounding Voices

Some perceived sounds have appeared to resemble voices
of personally familiar individuals.

The actual sources of these sounds have not been verified.

## 5.5 Apparent Multi-Speaker Conversations

At times, the sounds have appeared to involve multiple
participants engaged in coordinated dialogue.

This observation motivates the Synthetic Conversational
Simulation hypothesis.

## 5.6 Apparent Environmental Responsiveness

Some sounds have appeared temporally connected to activities
occurring nearby.

The existence and strength of any such relationship
require controlled testing.

## 5.7 Apparent Recording Changes

During file movement, copying, saving, or playback,
some audio characteristics have appeared to change
or disappear.

Potential explanations include:

- File conversion
- Lossy audio encoding
- Playback differences
- Automatic processing
- File corruption
- Changes in listening conditions
- Differences between original and processed files

These possibilities should be investigated through
file-integrity testing.

# 6. Proposed Operating Models

The investigation will compare several competing explanations.

## Model A: Conventional Acoustic Sources

Speech or speech-like sounds originate from ordinary
environmental acoustic sources.

## Model B: Electronic Interference

Electronic equipment unintentionally detects or reproduces
signals or electrical interference.

## Model C: Recording and Playback Artifacts

Microphone processing, gain, compression, encoding, or
playback introduces or emphasizes speech-like characteristics.

## Model D: Auditory Pattern Recognition

Ambiguous environmental sounds are perceived as recognizable
speech or familiar voices.

## Model E: Externally Generated Synthetic Audio

An identifiable external system generates artificial speech
that reaches the recording device or listener through
an established transmission mechanism.

## Model F: Radio-Frequency Auditory Mechanisms

A documented RF auditory mechanism is considered as a
research hypothesis, subject to applicable physical
constraints and independent measurements.

No model should be selected solely because it appears
consistent with an initial observation.

# 7. Evidence Collection Protocol

## 7.1 Required Incident Metadata

For every recording, document:

- Unique incident identifier
- Date
- Start and end time
- Time zone
- Geographic location
- Recording device
- Device model
- Operating system
- Recording application
- Microphone configuration
- Gain settings
- Sample rate
- Audio format
- Playback equipment
- Environmental conditions
- Nearby operating machinery
- Relevant observations

## 7.2 Recording Procedure

1. Record an environmental baseline.
2. Document the equipment operating nearby.
3. Capture audio using fixed settings.
4. Where possible, use two independent recording devices.
5. Record periods with relevant machinery operating.
6. Record comparison periods with machinery switched off.
7. Preserve the original recordings.
8. Conduct analysis only on working copies.

High-gain recording may be useful for exploring weak sounds,
but it should be accompanied by a normal-gain reference.

Increasing gain does not establish the source or meaning
of the amplified signal.

## 7.3 Independent Listening Tests

Use multiple listeners who have not been told what words
or voices they are expected to hear.

Record their interpretations independently.

Compare:

- Word agreement
- Speaker agreement
- Timing agreement
- Confidence ratings
- Differences between original and processed audio

## 7.4 File Integrity

Compute SHA-256 checksums immediately after acquiring
each original file.

Example Linux command:

    sha256sum recording_original.wav

Example Windows PowerShell command:

    Get-FileHash .\recording_original.wav -Algorithm SHA256

Maintain a manifest containing:

- File identifier
- Original filename
- File size
- Creation timestamp
- SHA-256 checksum
- Storage location
- Acquisition method
- Processing history

If a file appears to change, compare its checksum with
the original manifest.

A checksum difference establishes that file contents differ;
it does not, by itself, establish why they changed.

## 7.5 Preservation Requirements

Maintain:

- Original recordings
- Read-only evidence copies
- Working analysis copies
- Processing logs
- Software versions
- Checksum manifests
- Incident metadata
- Investigator notes

Do not overwrite source files.

# 8. Historical and Scientific Research

Relevant research topics include:

- Microwave auditory effects
- Radio-frequency hearing
- Electromagnetic interference
- Acoustic beamforming
- Directional sound
- Speech synthesis
- Voice cloning
- Conversational artificial intelligence
- Audio signal processing
- Auditory perception
- Human-computer interaction

Historical government research and patents may help establish
the development of particular technologies.

However, a patent, research contract, or technical publication
does not independently establish operational deployment
or involvement in a particular incident.

# 9. Legal and Investigative Considerations

The purpose of the evidence protocol is to preserve material
that may be evaluated by:

- Audio forensic specialists
- Electrical engineers
- RF engineers
- Digital forensic examiners
- Scientific researchers
- Legal professionals

Investigators should distinguish:

1. What was personally observed.
2. What was captured by an instrument.
3. What independent analysis confirms.
4. What technical mechanism could explain the result.
5. What evidence identifies a particular source.
6. What evidence, if any, establishes intentional conduct.
7. What documented damages can be connected to that conduct.

These distinctions are necessary when evaluating
potential legal claims.

# 10. Research Objectives

The project will pursue the following objectives:

1. Establish a standardized vocabulary for reported phenomena.
2. Preserve and organize original observations.
3. Develop repeatable recording procedures.
4. Identify objectively measurable audio characteristics.
5. Compare competing technical explanations.
6. Investigate relevant scientific publications and patents.
7. Evaluate whether observed patterns survive controlled testing.
8. Support independent technical review.
9. Produce documentation suitable for further scientific
   or legal evaluation.

# 11. Conclusion

I began this investigation because I encountered experiences
that I could not readily explain.

My intention is not to ask others to accept a conclusion
without supporting evidence.

Instead, I want to document what I have observed,
investigate the technologies that might be relevant,
and establish methods that others can independently test.

The potential misuse of synthetic speech and deceptive
audio deserves serious consideration because inaccurate
information can cause genuine harm.

At the same time, identifying the actual source of an
unusual sound requires careful investigation.

This manual is intended to evolve as additional
recordings, measurements, technical research, and
independent findings become available.

**An observation is the beginning of an investigation,
not the end of one.**

# Introduction

## Why I Began Investigating

I used to read accounts of what people called Voice-to-Skull (V2K) technology and think the concept was too far-fetched to take seriously. My perspective began changing when I noticed unusual experiences involving sounds, voices, environmental noise, and recordings that I could not readily explain. I began asking whether unfamiliar technologies might be relevant, while recognizing that an experience alone does not establish its explanation.

This account of the author's motivation is drawn from [RSH-001, “Introduction: Why I Started Investigating”](../research/001-voice-to-skull-v2k.md#1-introduction-why-i-started-investigating). It is presented as the author's stated motivation, not as evidence for a particular mechanism.

## Purpose of This Manual

The manual provides a structured place to document observations, examine research, compare testable explanations, preserve evidence, and support independent review. An unfamiliar experience can be recorded and investigated without either dismissing it or prematurely assigning a cause or responsible party.

Personal observations can be difficult to communicate as precisely as physical measurements. Careful descriptions, clearly marked uncertainty, preserved source material, and repeatable procedures help other people evaluate what was reported without being asked to accept an unsupported conclusion.

## Why Careful Attribution Matters

Synthetic or otherwise deceptive audio, voice impersonation, and misrepresentation could cause people to attribute statements to someone who did not make them. Decisions based on inaccurate information can contribute to false accusations, damaged relationships or reputations, distress, and escalation of conflict. These are potential consequences of misleading audio; they do not establish that any particular audio was fabricated or that a particular person or system was involved.

The central investigative question is whether perceived or recorded speech came from an identifiable human speaker, a recording, a synthetic source, an environmental sound, an electronic artifact, or another explanation. Claims about a source or intent require supporting evidence.

## Working Principles

- Record what was perceived or measured before interpreting it.
- Keep original observations distinct from analysis and hypotheses.
- Consider competing explanations, including ordinary acoustic, electronic, recording, and perceptual factors.
- Preserve source files and disclose missing information.
- Treat independent verification as a question to investigate, not an assumption.
